The Story in Brief

Jack Torrance, a recovering alcoholic and struggling writer, takes a job as winter caretaker of the isolated Overlook Hotel in the Colorado Rockies, bringing his wife Wendy and young son Danny. Danny has "the shining" — a psychic ability that lets him see things others cannot, including the hotel's violent history. As winter closes in and the hotel's supernatural presence begins to work on Jack's fragile sobriety and sanity, the family's survival comes under threat. Stephen King wrote the novel as a deeply personal reckoning with alcoholism and fatherhood. Stanley Kubrick made something colder and stranger — a film King famously despises, and one of the most studied horror films ever made.

Key Differences

Jack's arc

This is the central disagreement between the two works. King's Jack Torrance is a good man genuinely fighting his demons — his love for his family is real, his deterioration is tragic, and the hotel's evil is an external force that corrupts him. Kubrick's Jack Nicholson arrives already unhinged; there's nothing to corrupt because there's little left to lose. King finds this a betrayal of the novel's emotional core. He's right that something is lost — but Kubrick's version is doing something different, and arguably more disturbing.

Wendy Torrance

King's Wendy is a complex, resourceful woman with her own interiority and history. Kubrick reduces her to a trembling victim — Shelley Duvall's performance is committed but the character is written as passive throughout. King has cited this as one of his sharpest criticisms of the adaptation. On this point he has a strong case: the novel's Wendy is far more interesting than the film's.

The hotel as character

In the novel, the Overlook is explicitly malevolent — it wants Danny's power and uses Jack as a tool to get it. The haunting is real, specific, and rooted in the building's sordid history, which King details extensively. Kubrick leaves the supernatural ambiguous; it's never entirely clear whether the horror is real or a product of Jack's disintegrating mind. This ambiguity is what makes the film endlessly analysable and what makes it feel less like horror and more like dread.

Danny and the shining

The novel spends considerable time with Danny's perspective and his relationship with Tony, his imaginary friend and psychic alter ego. His gift is central to the plot's mechanics. In the film, Danny's shining is present but subordinated to Jack's story; the "REDRUM" sequence is iconic but the full scope of Danny's ability and its role in the hotel's interest in him is compressed considerably.

The ending

King's ending is cathartic: the hotel's boiler explodes, Jack dies, Danny and Wendy escape, and the evil is destroyed. It's a horror novel ending that honors the family's struggle. Kubrick's ending — Jack frozen in the hedge maze, the mysterious photograph placing him at a 1921 party — refuses catharsis entirely and opens into something inexplicable. The film's ending is more unsettling precisely because it resolves nothing.

Should You Read First?

Read first — but understand that you're reading a different story. King's novel is a character study about addiction and inherited violence, wrapped in a ghost story that plays entirely straight. Kubrick's film is an exercise in dread and ambiguity that uses the novel's skeleton to build something more abstract. Neither spoils the other because they're after different things. If you read first, you'll spend the film noticing what Kubrick changed and why. If you watch first, the novel will surprise you with how much warmer and more tragic it is.

Verdict

One of the rare cases where both versions are genuine masterworks and the argument between them is the point. King's novel is the better horror story — more humane, more frightening on its own terms, more interested in its characters as people. Kubrick's film is the more haunting cultural object, the one that lodges in the brain and refuses to leave. Read the book to understand what Kubrick left out. Watch the film to understand why he left it out.
