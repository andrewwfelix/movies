The Story in Brief

In a luxurious Kent retirement community called Coopers Chase, four septuagenarians meet every Thursday to investigate cold cases over tea and biscuits. Elizabeth, a former MI5 agent with ice in her veins and secrets in every pocket; Joyce, the warmhearted widow who chronicles their adventures in her diary; Ibrahim, a precise retired psychiatrist; and Ron, a gruff ex-union organizer with socialist fire still burning—together they form the Thursday Murder Club. When a local property developer turns up dead on the grounds, the quartet shift from armchair detection to a live murder investigation, aided—and occasionally hindered—by two local police detectives, the ambitious Donna De Freitas and her older partner Chris Hudson.

Richard Osman's debut novel became a publishing phenomenon by combining warmth, wit, and genuine mystery craft. The story juggles multiple timelines, including Elizabeth's enigmatic past and Joyce's blossoming friendship with a young drug dealer, while the central whodunit unspools with satisfying complexity. The book's real achievement is its refusal to condescend to its elderly protagonists or treat aging as a punchline—these are fully realized people with rich interior lives, regrets, romances, and agency.

Chris Columbus's 2026 adaptation brings Hollywood star power and glossy production values to the material, assembling a dream cast and filming on location in the UK. The film found a wide audience and charmed many critics, but the journey from page to screen required significant streamlining. What began as an intimate, digressive novel about friendship, memory, and mortality became a brisk, plot-driven caper that sacrifices texture for pace—a translation that works on its own terms but can't quite match the book's layered storytelling and emotional depth.

Key Differences

Elizabeth's Backstory Is Largely Erased

In the novel, Elizabeth's mysterious past as an MI5 operative provides crucial emotional ballast. Osman parcels out her history in carefully measured flashbacks—her recruitment, her morally ambiguous missions, her marriage to a fellow spy, and the painful secrets she's carried for decades. These passages aren't just narrative color; they're the book's emotional core, revealing how Elizabeth became the cool, controlled strategist we see in the present day. The mystery of her late husband Stephen, his connection to the murder, and her reckoning with choices made long ago give the story weight and poignancy. Joyce discovers Elizabeth's hidden file room, a moment that deepens their friendship and exposes Elizabeth's vulnerability.

The film, however, reduces Elizabeth's espionage career to a few throwaway lines and one brief, visually stylish flashback. Helen Mirren plays Elizabeth with appropriate steel and wit, but Columbus provides almost no context for her capabilities or inner life. The file room scene is cut entirely. Stephen exists only as a photograph and a name; his role in the murder plot is simplified and his emotional significance to Elizabeth barely registers. The result is that Mirren delivers a competent performance of a competent character, but the Elizabeth of the film is more archetype than person—the capable spymaster reduced to "older woman who is surprisingly badass," a trope that flatters without truly engaging.

Joyce's Relationship with the Drug Dealer Becomes Comic Relief

One of the book's most surprising and tender subplots involves Joyce befriending a young drug dealer who lives in her building. What begins as an unlikely pairing—the cheerful, chatty widow and the wary young man involved in Kent's criminal underworld—develops into a genuinely moving friendship. Osman lets this relationship breathe, showing how Joyce's non-judgmental warmth breaks through the young man's defenses and how he, in turn, offers her a window into a world she'd never encounter otherwise. The subplot connects thematically to the novel's exploration of unexpected connections and the ways people surprise us when we approach them with openness rather than assumption.

In Columbus's film, this relationship is compressed into three brief scenes played almost entirely for laughs. Joyce (Celia Imrie, delightful but underserved by the script) encounters the dealer, misunderstands drug slang in a scene designed for easy chuckles, and then he conveniently provides information that helps crack the case. There's no development, no emotional arc, no sense that these two people genuinely come to care for one another. The dealer himself is less a character than a device—he exists to generate a few giggles and then advance the plot. What the book treated with sincerity and nuance, the film treats as a comedic set piece, and the adaptation is poorer for it. It's emblematic of the film's broader tendency to sand down the book's emotional edges in favor of crowd-pleasing moments.

The Mystery's Structure Is Streamlined Into Linearity

Osman's novel braids together multiple mysteries: the present-day murder of developer Tony Curran; the cold case the club was investigating when Tony died; Elizabeth's past and its connection to both mysteries; and several red herrings involving other Coopers Chase residents. The book moves between timeframes and perspectives, trusts the reader to hold multiple threads, and rewards attention with a finale that ties disparate elements together. Crucially, the solution isn't just about identifying a killer—it's about understanding how past sins echo into the present, how institutional corruption enabled individual crimes, and how the four protagonists' different skills and backgrounds all prove essential to solving the case.

The film collapses this architecture into a single linear investigation. The cold case is mentioned but never explored; it doesn't factor into the solution. The timeline stays firmly in the present except for that one Elizabeth flashback. Several suspects from the book are eliminated entirely, and the conspiracy connecting Tony's murder to Elizabeth's past is simplified to the point of bluntness. Columbus and screenwriter Laura Dern construct the mystery efficiently—there are clues, there are suspects, there's a reveal—but the sense of accumulation and connection is lost. The movie's whodunit is competent but generic, the kind of puzzle you could slot into any cozy mystery framework. The book's mystery is specific to these characters, this place, these histories. That specificity is what elevates it from competent to memorable, and it's largely what the film sacrifices in the name of accessibility.

Ron and Ibrahim's Characterization Is Flattened

In the novel, Ron and Ibrahim are as fully developed as Elizabeth and Joyce. Ron's working-class socialism, his grief over his late wife, his prickly exterior and soft heart, his past as a union firebrand—all of this matters. Ibrahim's fastidiousness, his psychology background, his quiet observations, his own losses—Osman gives him space and dignity. Both men have arcs: Ron grapples with loneliness and slowly opens himself to new connection; Ibrahim confronts his own assumptions about aging and risk. The four members of the club feel like genuine equals, each bringing irreplaceable skills and perspective to the investigation.

The film reduces Ron and Ibrahim to sidekicks. Pierce Brosnan's Ron gets most of the good one-liners and a few gruff-but-lovable moments, but his backstory is sketched at best and his emotional journey is nonexistent. David Alan Grier's Ibrahim fares even worse—he's present in group scenes but rarely drives action or gets his own moments. The movie becomes the Elizabeth and Joyce show, with the two men functioning as support. Columbus likely made this choice for runtime and focus, but it diminishes the ensemble dynamic that makes the book special. The Thursday Murder Club novel argues that all four of these people matter equally, that a diversity of experience and skill is what makes them formidable. The film pays lip service to that idea but doesn't truly believe it, and you can feel the difference in every scene where Ron and Ibrahim fade into the background.

The Tone Shifts from Bittersweet to Purely Cozy

Osman's novel is fundamentally bittersweet. Yes, there's humor and warmth and the pleasure of watching clever people solve puzzles, but there's also real melancholy threading through every chapter. Characters are dying or have died; the residents of Coopers Chase are living in the shadow of mortality; Elizabeth is haunted by her past; Joyce is processing widowhood; the book acknowledges that these are people in the final chapters of their lives. Osman never lets the story become maudlin, but he doesn't shy away from the stakes either. The mystery plot gains gravity from this awareness—it matters that these four people are choosing to engage fully with life even as their time diminishes.

The film largely dispenses with this melancholy. Columbus, whose career has leaned toward crowd-pleasing sentiment (Home Alone, Harry Potter), directs The Thursday Murder Club as a feel-good romp. The elderly protagonists are spry and sharp, death is kept at arm's length, and the overall vibe is "aren't old people delightful when they solve crimes?" It's charming, to be sure—Mirren and Imrie have lovely chemistry, the supporting cast is game, and Columbus stages several genuinely funny set pieces. But charm isn't the same as depth. The film wants you to enjoy yourself without being troubled, to find the club's adventures purely entertaining rather than poignant. That's a defensible creative choice, but it means the adaptation loses the tonal complexity that made the book resonate so deeply with readers. You leave the film smiling; you leave the book smiling and thinking about mortality, friendship, and what it means to live fully. That difference matters.

Should You Read First?

Yes, absolutely. The film is pleasant enough—a well-cast, handsomely mounted mystery that will entertain you for two hours and then evaporate from memory. But the book is something richer and more lasting. Osman's prose is witty without being showy, his characters are drawn with specificity and affection, and his plotting balances genuine mystery craft with emotional payoff. The novel takes its time, trusts its readers, and earns its sentiment rather than simply reaching for it. If you watch the film first, you'll enjoy it fine but have little sense of what you're missing. If you read the book first, you'll appreciate what the film does well (the casting, mostly) while recognizing all the texture and depth that couldn't survive the adaptation process.

More importantly, the book establishes a relationship with these characters that the film can only gesture toward. By the time you finish the novel, you know Elizabeth, Joyce, Ron, and Ibrahim as people, not just as plot functions or charming archetypes. You understand their histories, their quirks, their vulnerabilities. That investment makes the sequels (yes, it's a series) even more rewarding. If you're going to spend time with the Thursday Murder Club, do it right: start with Osman's novel, let it work its bittersweet magic, and then, if you're curious, see what Hollywood made of it. You'll be glad you went in that order.

Verdict

The book wins, and it's not particularly close. Chris Columbus delivers a competent, charming adaptation that hits the major plot points and showcases a talented cast, but it's the cinematic equivalent of a good Wikipedia summary—you get the facts without the feeling. Osman's novel is warm, funny, surprisingly moving, and genuinely clever, a mystery that respects its genre while deepening it with real emotional stakes and thematic richness. The film is content to be a cozy caper; the book insists on being something more. Helen Mirren and company do their best, but they're working from a script that streamlines away most of what made the source material special. Read the book, enjoy the characters, savor the prose, and feel the melancholy underneath the wit. Then, if you must, watch the film and appreciate it as a pleasant but ultimately unnecessary adaptation. The Thursday Murder Club on the page is the version worth your time.
