The Story in Brief

Flora Poste is a recently orphaned, expensively educated young woman of the 1930s with very clear opinions about how things ought to be done. Rather than work for a living, she decides to impose herself on distant relatives — the Starkadders of Cold Comfort Farm in rural Sussex, a family of magnificent dysfunction governed by the terrifying Aunt Ada Doom, who saw something nasty in the woodshed and has never recovered. Flora sets about organising everyone with brisk good cheer. Stella Gibbons's 1932 debut novel is a pitch-perfect parody of the rural Gothic novels that dominated English fiction in the 1920s — Mary Webb and D.H. Lawrence particularly — delivered in a prose of immaculate deadpan. John Schlesinger's 1995 BBC film adaptation stars Kate Beckinsale as Flora and a supporting cast of considerable distinction.

Key Differences

Gibbons's prose style

The comedy of the novel exists entirely in Gibbons's prose — the perfectly calibrated gap between the overwrought Gothic language she uses to describe the Starkadders and the cheerful rationality with which Flora responds to them. Gibbons marked the novel's most purple passages with asterisks, as a parody of the critical apparatus that greeted serious rural fiction, and this meta-layer is entirely lost on screen. The film captures the situation's comedy but not the comedy of the prose itself — the humour of seeing Starkadder-level melodrama described in Flora's sensible terms.

Kate Beckinsale as Flora

Beckinsale is well-cast — Flora requires a quality of serene, cheerful competence that is never smug, and Beckinsale finds this register precisely. She makes Flora's certainty likeable rather than insufferable, which is the role's central challenge. The novel's Flora is perhaps more satirically self-aware — she knows she is imposing order on chaos and finds the whole enterprise amusing as well as purposeful. Beckinsale's Flora is warmer and slightly less ironic.

Aunt Ada Doom

Eileen Atkins plays Aunt Ada — the matriarch who has kept the family in thrall for decades through the threat of going completely mad — with magnificent theatrical commitment. The character is inherently performative and Atkins understands this completely. The novel's Aunt Ada is somewhat more menacing precisely because she is described rather than shown; the film's version is more immediately funny. Both work.

Ian McKellen as Amos

McKellen's Amos Starkadder — the fire-and-brimstone preacher who secretly yearns to drive a motor vehicle across America — is the film's comic highlight. His sermon sequence, in which Amos harangues his congregation with terrifying intensity about the fate of sinners, is one of the great comic performances in British television. The novel's Amos is the satirical target; McKellen's is a genuine creation.

The satirical targets

In 1932, Gibbons's targets — the rural Gothic novel, the idea that peasant life contained a deeper authenticity than modern urban existence, the prose style of writers like Mary Webb — were immediately recognisable to her readers. By 1995, these targets required some explanation. Schlesinger's film wisely does not explain them; it plays the satire straight and lets audiences respond to the comedy of situation without needing to know what is being parodied. This means some of the novel's specific wit is lost, but the general comedy survives intact.

Should You Read First?

Yes — Gibbons's prose is the experience and the film's comedy, while genuine, is shallower than the novel's. Read first to encounter the asterisked passages, the specific parody targets, and Flora's particular brand of cheerful rationalism in its full written glory. Then watch the film for McKellen's sermon and Atkins's Ada, which are pleasures the novel cannot provide.

Verdict

Gibbons wrote one of British literature's great comic novels — a precision satire of rural Gothic fiction in a prose of immaculate deadpan wit. Schlesinger's BBC film is warm, well-cast, and captures the comedy of situation without fully replicating the comedy of style. The novel is the richer experience. The film is an excellent companion. Read first, then watch McKellen preach. Both are essential.
