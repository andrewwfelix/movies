The Story in Brief

Call me Ishmael. A young man signs aboard the whaling ship Pequod under the command of Captain Ahab — a man consumed by his determination to hunt and kill the white sperm whale that took his leg. The voyage becomes a meditation on obsession, fate, God, and the nature of evil, interspersed with extended chapters on the anatomy of whales, the economics of the whaling industry, the technology of harpooning, and the philosophy of whiteness. Herman Melville's 1851 novel is one of the most ambitious works in American literature — a book that contains everything and resists summary. John Huston's 1956 film, with a screenplay by Ray Bradbury and Gregory Peck as Ahab, extracts the plot and delivers it with craft and conviction. It cannot deliver the novel.

Key Differences

The digressions are the novel

Melville interrupts the narrative repeatedly with chapters that have nothing to do with plot — extended technical treatises on whale anatomy, meditations on the colour white, the economics of the sperm whale oil trade, the history of whaling. These chapters are not padding; they are the novel's argument. By teaching the reader everything about whales, Melville makes the whale's destruction feel genuinely significant — you understand what is being destroyed and why the obsession to destroy it is madness. Huston's film, necessarily, omits all of this. The whale becomes a symbol without the substance that makes it a symbol worth pursuing.

Gregory Peck as Ahab

Peck was not Huston's first choice — he wanted Orson Welles — and some critics found his Ahab too handsome, too controlled, lacking the terrifying charisma Melville describes. Others find his performance a genuine achievement of restraint and authority. The debate is worth having. Peck's Ahab is a man of terrible purpose; Melville's is something closer to a force of nature that happens to be wearing a man's face.

Ishmael's narration

The novel is narrated by Ishmael — the sole survivor, writing after the fact — and his voice ranges from colloquial to philosophical to comic to tragic, sometimes within a single chapter. This range is the novel's tonal signature and one of its great pleasures. The film reduces Ishmael to a functional observer, present but not narrating. Richard Basehart plays him with dignity; Melville's Ishmael is something more eccentric and more alive.

The crew

Melville's Pequod carries a deliberately international crew — a microcosm of the world's peoples, each with their own relationship to the hunt. Queequeg, the tattooed harpooner who becomes Ishmael's close companion, is one of literature's great characters, and his friendship with Ishmael is the novel's emotional counterweight to Ahab's mania. The film's crew is present but the depth of characterisation that Melville lavishes on them is necessarily compressed.

The whale itself

Moby Dick appears rarely in the novel and the encounters, when they come, have a quality of apocalyptic strangeness — the whale is described but never fully seen, never fully known. The 1956 film's practical whale is less convincing by modern standards, but Huston understood the right approach: the whale should feel enormous and inexplicable. The climactic three-day chase is the film's strongest sequence.

Should You Read First?

Read as much of the novel as you can — even if the digressive chapters slow you down, push through them, because they are the argument the plot is making. See the film as a skilled reduction of that argument to its narrative essentials. The novel is one of the great reading experiences in American literature. The film is a useful companion for those who find the novel's full scope daunting.

Verdict

Melville wrote a novel that contains a whaling encyclopaedia, a philosophical meditation on obsession and fate, a tragedy, a comedy, and one of literature's great friendships — all within the same covers. Huston made a handsome, stripped-down film of the plot. The novel is the thing. Read it, or as much of it as you can manage. See the film for Gregory Peck's Ahab, who carries the obsession with genuine authority even if he cannot carry the whole book.
