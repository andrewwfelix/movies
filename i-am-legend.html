The Story in Brief

Robert Neville is the last uninfected man on Earth. A pandemic has turned the rest of humanity into vampires — creatures that gather outside his fortified house each night, calling his name, waiting. By day he hunts them. By night he drinks and tries not to go mad. Richard Matheson's 1954 novella is one of the foundational texts of modern horror — it influenced Stephen King, George Romero's zombie films, and effectively invented the post-apocalyptic survival genre. Francis Lawrence's 2007 film, with Will Smith giving one of his finest performances, grossed $585 million worldwide. It is a gripping, technically accomplished film with the wrong ending.

Key Differences

The ending — the whole point

Matheson's novella ends with a revelation that reframes everything that came before: the vampires have developed a functioning society, and Neville — who has spent years hunting and killing them in their sleep — is the monster from their perspective. The legend of the title is what Neville has become to the new civilisation: the boogeyman, the night-stalker, the creature of nightmare. It is one of the great reversals in genre fiction. The 2007 film's theatrical ending replaces this entirely with Neville dying heroically to save survivors, turning a story about the relativity of monstrosity into a conventional sacrifice narrative. An alternate ending that is closer to Matheson's was shot but not used in the theatrical release.

The vampires as society

Matheson's vampires are not mindless — they are frightened, organised, and building something new. The leader Ben Cortman was Neville's neighbour, and his nightly appearances outside Neville's house have a desperate human quality beneath the horror. The film's infected are rendered as purely predatory CGI creatures with no visible interiority, which makes them effective as horror but removes the novella's central argument about who deserves to survive.

Will Smith alone

The film's considerable achievement is its extended early sequence of Smith as Neville alone in a deserted New York — hunting deer through empty streets, talking to mannequins, maintaining military discipline to avoid psychological collapse. These sequences are among the finest survival cinema of the 2000s. Smith carries the film on his presence alone, and the performance is genuinely moving in ways the script does not fully deserve.

Neville's scientific research

The novella's Neville is an ordinary man who teaches himself biology from library books — his slow, amateur scientific work is part of the novel's texture. The film makes him a military virologist, which makes his research more conventionally credible and less interesting. The novel's Neville succeeds through persistence and ordinary intelligence; the film's through professional expertise.

Sam the dog

Both versions feature Neville's dog — his only living companion. The film's handling of Sam's fate is the most emotionally devastating scene in either version and is more fully developed than Matheson's equivalent. This is one of the rare cases where the film invests more in a specific moment than the source, and it pays off completely.

Should You Read First?

Yes — and read the novella specifically to experience the ending that the film removes. The theatrical cut of the 2007 film actively contradicts Matheson's argument. Read first, then watch the alternate ending version of the film rather than the theatrical cut. The novella takes two hours and contains one of genre fiction's great last lines.

Verdict

Matheson wrote a novella with one of the great endings in horror fiction — a final inversion that reframes everything that came before. The 2007 film discards this ending entirely, replacing it with a conventional heroic conclusion that misses the point completely. Will Smith is excellent. The film is gripping. And it tells a fundamentally different story to the one Matheson wrote. Read the novella. It takes two hours and the last line will stay with you.
