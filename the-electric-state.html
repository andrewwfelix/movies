The Story in Brief

In an alternate 1997, a teenage girl named Michelle drives across the southwestern United States in a stolen car, accompanied by a small, strange robot. She is searching for her younger brother, who may or may not still exist. The world around her is littered with the ruins of consumer technology, failed virtual reality experiments, and the detritus of a society that collapsed under the weight of its own inventions. Simon Stålenhag's 2018 novel is an illustrated masterpiece — equal parts road trip, ghost story, and meditation on loneliness, memory, and the seduction of escape. The Russo Brothers' 2025 Netflix adaptation expands the world, adds action set pieces, and brings the story to a much larger audience. Both versions share the same premise, but their tones and ambitions differ significantly.

Key Differences

Visual Storytelling & Atmosphere

Stålenhag's novel is famous for its stunning, melancholic paintings — one on nearly every page. The art does as much storytelling as the prose, conveying a sense of vast, lonely desolation and quiet horror. The film, by necessity, replaces these still images with motion and spectacle. While the Russo Brothers are skilled visual directors, the book's unique ability to linger on a single haunting image is something cinema cannot replicate. The film is likely to be faster, louder, and more action-oriented — trading atmosphere for pacing.

Plot Expansion & New Characters

The novel is lean, impressionistic, and sparse on dialogue. It follows Michelle almost exclusively, with long stretches of silence and internal reflection. Early reports indicate the film adds a larger cast (including Chris Pratt's character, who does not exist in the book), subplots involving government conspiracies, and action sequences that break the quiet tension of the road trip. These additions will make the film more commercial but dilute the novel's lonely, focused voice.

Tone — Hopelessness vs. Hope

Stålenhag's world is bleak and resigned. The collapse has already happened; there is no fixing it. Michelle's journey is less about saving anyone and more about bearing witness. Studio films, especially big-budget Netflix productions, tend to lean toward hope and heroism. Expect the film to soften the book's nihilistic edges, add a clearer "mission" for Michelle, and likely provide a more uplifting ending. The novel's ambiguous, melancholic finale is beautiful but not mainstream-friendly.

The Robot (Skip)

Michelle's robot companion, named Skip in both versions, is a central figure. In the book, Skip is damaged, quiet, and communicates mostly through gestures and simple sounds — his mystery is part of the power. The film will almost certainly give Skip more personality, possibly voice (or voiceover from a celebrity), and make him a more traditional sidekick. This may make Skip more lovable but less eerie.

The Ending

The novel's ending is deliberately unresolved — we are never entirely sure what happened to Michelle's brother, or what the "electric state" really is. The film will almost certainly provide concrete answers, a clearer resolution, and possibly a heroic sacrifice or reunion. The book trusts the reader to sit with ambiguity; the film will trust the audience to enjoy a satisfying conclusion.

Should You Read First?

Yes — especially for this adaptation. The Electric State is a short, immersive read (under 150 pages of prose plus illustrations) that you can finish in an afternoon. The book's atmosphere, its haunting paintings, and its quiet, sad, beautiful tone are the entire point. The film will be a different beast: bigger, faster, and more commercial. Reading first will let you experience Stålenhag's original vision before the Russo Brothers remake it in their image. If you see the film first, you may find the book too slow and too quiet. If you read the book first, you will appreciate what the film adds — and mourn what it loses.

Verdict

The Electric State is a rare case where the book is almost certainly the superior artistic achievement, but the film will reach millions more people. Stålenhag's novel is a quiet masterpiece — a perfect marriage of prose and painting that creates a world unlike any other. The Russo Brothers' film will be entertaining, well-made, and visually impressive, but it cannot replicate the book's lonely, melancholy soul. Read the book first. Savor it. Then see what Hollywood does with it. The book wins for atmosphere, originality, and emotional resonance — but the film may still be worth your time as a companion piece.
