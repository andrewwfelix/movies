The Masters of the Craft:
Top 20 Film Critics Ranked

From the "thumbs up" era to the digital frontier, these are the voices that provided more than just a score—they provided a lens through which to see the world.

01
The Populist Poet
Roger Ebert

Roger Ebert is the gold standard because he wrote for the reader, not just for the industry. His brilliance lay in his ability to be profoundly intellectual while remaining entirely accessible, treating movies as "empathy machines" that could improve the human condition. He understood that a review wasn't just a verdict; it was a conversation between two people who loved the same thing.

02
The Stylist
Pauline Kael

Pauline Kael transformed film criticism into a visceral, slangy, and intensely personal art form. She didn't believe in "objectivity," choosing instead to write with a fervor that made you feel the movie in your bones. Her influence at The New Yorker was so vast that she single-handedly championed the "New Hollywood" movement of the 70s, proving that a critic could be as much of a rockstar as the directors they covered.

03
The Intellectual
James Agee

Before film was widely considered a "serious" art, James Agee was writing about it with the reverence of a theologian and the precision of a poet. His reviews in The Nation during the 1940s established the standard for film writing, blending a deep love for the medium with a rigorous moral and aesthetic scrutiny that had never been seen before.

04
The Contrarian
Andrew Sarris

Sarris is the man who brought "Auteur Theory" to America, forever changing how we view the role of the director. By arguing that a film's true meaning came from the distinct personality of its creator, he gave us a new vocabulary to appreciate cinema. He wasn't afraid to be wrong, but he was almost always fascinating to read.

05
The Modern Dean
A.O. Scott

For over two decades at The New York Times, A.O. Scott has provided a steady, erudite, and deeply thoughtful voice in a chaotic digital landscape. He has an uncanny ability to connect a single film to the broader cultural and political zeitgeist, making his reviews feel like essential dispatches on the state of the world.

06
The Tactician
Manny Farber

Manny Farber was the champion of "termite art"—small, focused, and unpretentious films that did their work quietly. He loathed "white elephant art" (the bloated prestige films that usually win Oscars) and taught an entire generation how to look for beauty in the margins and the shadows of B-movies.

07
The Grump
Gene Siskel

Often overshadowed by his partner Ebert, Siskel was a brilliant critic in his own right, known for his acerbic wit and his refusal to be charmed by sentimentality. He asked the toughest question a critic can ask: "Is this movie better than a documentary of the same actors having lunch?" It’s a standard that still holds up today.

08
The Curator
Jonathan Rosenbaum

Rosenbaum spent his career at the Chicago Reader fighting against the "media-industrial complex." He is perhaps the greatest champion of international and experimental cinema, constantly pushing his readers to look beyond the local multiplex to find the true avant-garde of the art form.

09
The Firebrand
Armond White

While often called a "troll" for his contrarian takes, Armond White is a vital critic because he forces his readers to defend their assumptions. His reviews are deeply rooted in African-American culture and a rigorous understanding of film history, often finding profound meaning in blockbusters that others dismiss as trash.

10
The Observer
Manohla Dargis

A co-lead critic at the New York Times, Dargis writes with a fierce intelligence and a sharp eye for visual grammar. She is particularly skilled at dissecting the gender dynamics and power structures within a film, making her reviews essential reading for anyone interested in the social impact of cinema.

11
The Prose Stylist
Anthony Lane

Writing for The New Yorker, Lane is widely considered the funniest film critic alive. His reviews are masterpieces of drollery and wit, often containing sentences so perfectly constructed they are more entertaining than the movies he is reviewing. He reminds us that criticism, at its best, should be a pleasure to read.

12
The Historian
David Thomson

Thomson’s "Biographical Dictionary of Film" is the bible of the industry, but his reviews are equally legendary. He writes about movies with a sense of melancholic nostalgia, treating the history of cinema as a long, shared dream that we are all slowly waking up from.

13
The Digital Voice
Amy Nicholson

Representing the best of the modern era, Amy Nicholson brings a fresh, energetic, and deeply researched perspective to film criticism. Whether in print or on her various podcasts, she excels at contextualizing movies within the lives of the people who made them, humanizing the industry in a way few others do.

14
The Academic
Robin Wood

Wood was a pioneer in applying serious academic rigor to genres previously considered "disposable," particularly horror. His analysis of directors like Alfred Hitchcock and Howard Hawks helped legitimize the study of Hollywood cinema, proving that even a slasher flick could contain deep psychological truths.

15
The Guardian
Mark Kermode

The UK’s most prominent critic, Kermode is known for his passionate rants and his unwavering integrity. He is a staunch defender of the theatrical experience and medium-sized movies, providing a much-needed balance to a world increasingly dominated by streaming and superhero sequels.

16
The Satirist
Walter Chaw

Writing for Film Freak Central, Chaw is one of the most uncompromising voices in criticism. His reviews are often dark, deeply personal, and unflinchingly honest about the ways movies fail us—and the ways they can occasionally save us.

17
The Moralist
Vincent Canby

Canby was the face of New York Times film criticism during the transition from the old studio system to the auteur-driven 70s. He had a reputation for being tough but fair, with an elegant prose style that never felt the need to shout to be heard.

18
The Enthusiast
Elvis Mitchell

Mitchell is a critic with a legendary "ear." His reviews and interviews focus heavily on the rhythm, music, and spoken word of cinema. He brings an infectious energy to his work, making you want to watch even the movies he ends up panning.

19
The Scholar
B. Ruby Rich

As the woman who coined the term "New Queer Cinema," Rich is a towering figure in independent film criticism. She has spent her career championing voices that the mainstream ignored, proving that the best critics are those who can spot a movement before it even has a name.

20
The Outsider
J. Hoberman

Longtime critic for The Village Voice, Hoberman viewed film primarily through a political and historical lens. He was a master at connecting the images on the screen to the hidden anxieties of the American public, making every review feel like a piece of high-level sociopolitical analysis.
