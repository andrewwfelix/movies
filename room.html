The Story in Brief

Jack is five years old and has never left Room — an eleven-by-eleven-foot garden shed where he was born and where his mother, held captive by a man he knows only as Old Nick, has raised him with fierce love and radical ingenuity. To Jack, Room is the entire world; everything on television is make-believe. To his mother, it is a prison she has survived for seven years. Emma Donoghue's novel is narrated entirely by Jack, a formal choice of tremendous risk and equal reward. Lenny Abrahamson's film, with a screenplay by Donoghue herself, is one of the finest adaptations of the decade — and the two versions are close enough in quality to make this one of the hardest calls on this site.

Key Differences

Jack's narration

The novel's entire architecture rests on Jack's five-year-old voice — his limited vocabulary, his concrete thinking, his total lack of irony. Donoghue sustains this for over three hundred pages without condescension or cuteness, and the gap between what Jack understands and what the reader understands is where the novel's horror and tenderness both live. Jack Tremblay's performance in the film is one of the great child performances in cinema, but voiceover can only gesture at what full first-person narration delivers. The novel's Jack is a formal triumph; the film's Jack is a human one.

The escape sequence

Both versions handle the escape with real tension, but the film has a visceral advantage: watching Jack rolled in a rug, tipped into a truck bed, and running toward a stranger on a suburban street is almost unbearable in real time. The novel builds the same tension through Jack's limited understanding of what is happening — he doesn't fully grasp the danger, which makes it more frightening, not less. Two different kinds of dread, both effective. The film's escape may be the single most gripping sequence adapted from any book on this site.

Ma's breakdown

Brie Larson won the Academy Award for Best Actress and earned every frame of it. The film gives her Ma's collapse after escape — the media intrusion, the failed interview, the suicide attempt — with devastating clarity. The novel filters all of this through Jack's incomplete comprehension, which creates a different and equally powerful effect: you watch a child trying to understand his mother's pain with the tools a five-year-old has available. The book's version is more original; the film's version is more immediately heartbreaking.

The world outside

Jack's adjustment to the outside world — its scale, its noise, its social complexity — is the novel's richest material. Donoghue takes her time with his sensory overwhelm, his attachment difficulties, his gradual renegotiation of what is real. The film compresses this section more than any other, moving toward resolution faster than the book does. The novel's second half is as strong as its first; the film's second half is slightly less assured than its extraordinary first hour.

The ending

Both versions end with Jack saying goodbye to Room — a scene of unexpected emotional complexity, in which a child grieves the only home he has ever known even as he understands it was a prison. The film renders this visually with great restraint. The novel earns it more fully because you have spent the entire book inside Jack's relationship with that space — his naming of its objects, his routines, his love for it. When he says goodbye on the page, you feel the loss of something that was genuinely his, however terrible its origins.

Should You Read First?

This is the one case on this site where watching first is a defensible choice. The film is so good, and Donoghue's screenplay so faithful to her own novel's spirit, that either order works. That said, reading first gives you Jack's narration in full — and that narration is one of the more remarkable formal achievements in recent fiction. Read first if you want the fuller experience. Watch first if you want to be destroyed immediately and then find out how the book does it differently.

Verdict

Room is the rare adaptation where both versions are essential and neither fully supersedes the other. The novel's first-person narration is irreplaceable; the film's performances are unrepeatable. Larson and Tremblay do things on screen that the page cannot do, and Donoghue on the page does things the screen cannot replicate. See both. Start with whichever you prefer — you won't regret either order.
